# Flat 18 competitive strategy roadmap

**Updated:** 29 August 2026  
**Basis:** review of Flat 18's current offering and the closest competing firms: Foundry 5, Coderacle, The Random Stuff and devkyn.

## Strategic conclusion

Flat 18's delivery capability is not the main weakness. The bigger gap is the way that capability is presented and discovered.

The strongest competitors make four things obvious very quickly:

1. What the buyer can buy.
2. Why the work will be delivered quickly.
3. Why the result will still be reliable and maintainable.
4. What the buyer should do next.

The recommended position for Flat 18 is therefore:

> Flat 18 turns rough, fast-built and AI-built prototypes into trustworthy production software.

LLMs are the acceleration layer. Senior developers remain responsible for the product decisions, architecture, review and release.

## What the competitor research showed

### Foundry 5

Foundry 5 appears ahead on authority, founder distribution, visible proof and risk reversal. The lesson for Flat 18 is to make experience easier to believe: show the work, explain the decisions and reduce the perceived risk of choosing a small studio.

### Coderacle

Coderacle appears ahead on organic search, pricing architecture and scale signals. The lesson is to give each high-intent buyer a clear route into the site, with useful pages that answer the exact buying question rather than relying on one broad agency homepage.

### The Random Stuff

The Random Stuff is strong at productising the offer. Its subscription, queue and qualification model are easy to understand. The lesson is to make the engagement model feel like a deliberate product with clear boundaries, not a collection of possible services.

### devkyn

devkyn appears strong on long-tail acquisition and explaining an ongoing senior development subscription. The lesson is to build search-led pages around real problems and explain how ongoing work operates in plain language.

## Changes already made

### 1. Sharper homepage positioning

The hero now leads with:

> Ship better products faster with expert use of LLMs and AI

The supporting line names the actual outputs: polished MVPs, dashboards and full-stack systems. This replaces the broader “digital product agency” description as the primary commercial message.

### 2. Clearer trust argument

The homepage now foregrounds “AI-led. Senior-built.” and keeps the three practical proof points visible:

- Senior control: architecture, security and release stay in expert hands.
- Weeks, not months: LLMs speed up drafts, tests, documentation and build decisions.
- Code you own: the repository, roadmap and technical decisions are included.

The existing “AI speed. Senior control.” section remains the short answer to the main objection: using AI does not mean shipping raw or disposable output.

### 3. Stronger work proof

The work section now says “Work that proves it” and “Real product builds, not pitch-deck theatre.” It uses a stable set of three product examples rather than changing the examples on each page load.

This makes the first proof encounter more deliberate and gives future case-study links a consistent role in the buying journey.

### 4. Productised engagement routes

The services section now leads with “Three ways to build” and uses direct route names:

- MVP Sprint — focused first versions.
- Product Build — serious end-to-end launches.
- Monthly Studio — ongoing product work.

The pricing section now uses “Pricing for serious product work” and positions the entry project route as a “Curated MVP Sprint”.

### 5. A visible, controlled process

The homepage now includes the missing process proof:

1. Scope — turn the idea into a buildable plan.
2. Draft — use LLMs to explore UI, code and documentation options.
3. Build — shape, test and connect the product with senior developers.
4. Review — check quality, security and release readiness.

This is designed to communicate speed with control, rather than speed as uncontrolled generation.

### 6. A more useful Tiny Audit entry point

The Tiny Audit strip now promises three practical fixes and what to do next. The contact form has been kept deliberately light: name, email and one project description. Visitors can include any useful context in that description without being asked to classify the work first.

This turns the audit from a vague call to action into a concrete low-friction acquisition offer.

### 7. Clearer navigation and search signals

Process is now a first-class navigation item. Homepage metadata, structured data, category and industry descriptions now emphasise MVP development, full-stack product engineering and dashboard development.

The page keeps the relevant terms—MVP development, AI-assisted development, LLM product development, senior developers, product design and web app development—without broadening the title into a list of every service.

### 8. Less distraction on the main route

The technology-logo section has been removed from the homepage conversion sequence. The stack remains useful on service and supporting pages, but the homepage now spends its attention on buyer questions: fit, proof, trust, route, process and next step.

### 9. A focused prototype-rescue acquisition route

The new `/prototype-rescue` page gives teams with an AI-built or fast-built prototype a direct route into the site. It explains the production-hardening problem, shows the three-stage response, links relevant work, answers common objections and points to the AI-assisted development service.

The route is included in the build-route grid and sitemap, with its own search metadata and structured data.

## Next changes

The next phase should focus on turning the sharper position into a repeatable acquisition system.

### Priority 1 — Make proof more commercial

Upgrade the strongest case studies so each one quickly answers:

- What was unclear, fragile or blocked?
- What did Flat 18 design and build?
- What changed for the product or team?
- What evidence can a buyer inspect?

Start with Ledger, SignalMap, Social Publisher and one dashboard or finance example. Add a short summary, product type, delivery role, relevant screenshots, technology context and a direct CTA back to the matching route.

### Priority 2 — Build the remaining high-intent acquisition pages

Create the remaining focused pages for the problems most likely to produce qualified enquiries:

- MVP development for founders.
- Dashboard and internal tool development.
- Full-stack product development for small teams.
- Ongoing senior product development support.

Each page should have one audience, one problem, one offer, one proof set and one CTA. Link these pages from the relevant case studies, services and contact flow.

### Priority 3 — Finish the pricing architecture

The homepage now names three routes, but the commercial model still exposes two main pricing paths. Decide whether the public offer should be:

- A fixed Curated MVP Sprint with a clear starting scope and timeline.
- A larger Product Build sold through a qualified proposal.
- A Monthly Studio route with capacity, queue rules, pause terms and a clear fit test.

Then make the homepage, `/pricing`, contact form options, payment flow and structured data use the same names and expectations.

### Priority 4 — Turn the Tiny Audit into a real funnel

Give the audit its own small landing page or dedicated state in the contact flow. Define:

- What the visitor submits.
- What the three fixes include.
- The response time.
- The boundary between a useful audit and paid product work.
- The CTA after the reply.

Track audit starts, links submitted, replies sent and audit-to-project conversations separately from general contact enquiries.

### Priority 5 — Add search-led content and comparison pages

Use the competitor gap around long-tail acquisition to publish useful, specific pages rather than generic blog posts. Good first topics include:

- How to take an AI-built prototype into production.
- When to rebuild an MVP instead of adding more features.
- How much an MVP or dashboard build costs.
- Senior developer subscription versus a fixed-scope build.

Comparison pages should be factual and useful. They should explain the trade-offs between fixed projects, subscriptions and internal hiring, then make Flat 18's fit clear without attacking named firms.

### Priority 6 — Strengthen trust and measurement

Add the proof that the leading competitors use to reduce uncertainty:

- Named testimonials where permission exists.
- Client or product logos where permission exists.
- Delivery timelines and engagement boundaries.
- A plain-English FAQ about AI use, code ownership, security and handover.
- Conversion events for route selection, pricing engagement, audit submission and contact submission.

## Suggested order of work

1. Upgrade four case studies and connect them to the three build routes.
2. Finalise the public pricing model and align every route name.
3. Launch the Tiny Audit landing flow.
4. Publish the five high-intent acquisition pages.
5. Add comparison and cost-explainer content.
6. Add testimonials, FAQs and the fuller conversion reporting layer.

## Success standard

Within 30 seconds, a new visitor should understand:

1. Flat 18 turns rough ideas and prototypes into trustworthy products.
2. The work covers MVPs, dashboards and full-stack systems.
3. LLMs create speed while senior developers control quality.
4. There is visible work to inspect.
5. There is a clear route to buy.
6. The Tiny Audit is an easy next step if the visitor is not ready to commit.
