# Flat 18 social presence action plan

**Updated:** 3 September 2026

This is the practical follow-through from the review of Flat 18 and four direct competitors: Foundry 5, Coderacle, The Random Stuff and devkyn.

## What to take from the research

The useful pattern is not posting more for its own sake. It is making the work easier to believe.

- **Foundry 5:** show named work, decisions and outcomes. A small studio can look established when its proof is specific.
- **Coderacle:** make the route and price logic easy to understand. Buyers should know what kind of engagement they are considering before they enquire.
- **The Random Stuff:** productise the offer and collect visible third-party feedback. Clear boundaries reduce uncertainty.
- **devkyn:** build useful pages around real buyer problems and explain how ongoing work operates.

Flat 18 should combine those lessons around one point of view: we use AI to move quickly, while senior developers remain responsible for the product, code and release.

## Changes now made in the site

1. The homepage now keeps Social Publisher, SignalMap and Ledger as a deliberate, stable proof set. The cards already expose focus, evidence and outcome where the project record supports it.
2. The work section now links directly to build notes on LinkedIn.

## Publishing rhythm

Use one useful post each week for six weeks. Each post should contain one clear idea, one piece of evidence and one low-pressure route back to the site. A screenshot, short screen recording or diagram is more valuable than a polished generic graphic.

Suggested weekly pattern:

1. A project decision or before-and-after.
2. A short lesson from taking a rough or AI-built prototype towards production.
3. A practical explanation of one service route or Tiny Audit question.
4. A case-study detail that shows how the product works.
5. A plain-English technical or product trade-off.
6. A direct invitation to share a prototype for review.

## Ready-to-draft post ideas

These are prompts, not claims to publish unchanged. Add a real screenshot, recording, quote or artefact before posting.

### 1. The work after the prototype

**Hook:** An AI-built prototype can look finished long before it is ready to support real users.

**Point:** The next work is usually about unclear states, missing failure paths, permissions, data shape, testing and release decisions.

**Evidence:** Show one real example from a Flat 18 prototype-rescue or product build.

**CTA:** “Have a prototype that needs a second pass? We can review it through a Tiny Audit.”

### 2. Social Publisher: from scattered checks to one workflow

**Hook:** Publishing content is rarely just a calendar problem.

**Point:** Channel variants, validation, scheduling, logs and retries need to sit in one workflow if a team is going to trust it.

**Evidence:** Show the relevant Social Publisher screen and name the decision it supports.

**CTA:** Link to the Social Publisher case study.

### 3. SignalMap: useful analytics without a noisy dashboard

**Hook:** More analytics does not automatically create more clarity.

**Point:** SignalMap turns privacy-limited browser signals into aggregate reporting and practical recommendations.

**Evidence:** Show one recommendation and the source signal behind it.

**CTA:** Link to the SignalMap case study.

### 4. Ledger: trust is a product feature

**Hook:** A shared money record is only useful if people can explain where the number came from.

**Point:** Receipt-backed records and read-only sharing make everyday balances easier to check and discuss.

**Evidence:** Show the receipt or read-only review flow.

**CTA:** Link to the Ledger case study.

### 5. One decision before adding another feature

**Hook:** Before adding the next feature, ask which uncertainty it removes.

**Point:** A feature should clarify a user decision, remove a repeated manual step or make a risky state safer. Otherwise it may be adding surface area rather than product value.

**Evidence:** Use a real backlog or interface decision, with client-sensitive details removed.

**CTA:** Invite readers to share the decision they are stuck on.

### 6. What a Tiny Audit actually checks

**Hook:** A Tiny Audit is not a vague “strategy call”.

**Point:** It is a focused review of an existing prototype or early product, ending in three practical fixes and a clear next step. The current offer is £500 with a three-working-day timeline.

**Evidence:** Show the structure of a redacted audit output, never a client’s private details.

**CTA:** Link to `/tiny-audit`.

### 7. The difference between fast and rushed

**Hook:** Fast delivery and rushed delivery are not the same thing.

**Point:** Fast work has a short feedback loop, visible decisions and a review before release. Rushed work hides uncertainty until it becomes expensive.

**Evidence:** Show a small scope, review or release checklist from a real build.

**CTA:** Link to the relevant service route.

### 8. A production-readiness question

**Hook:** What happens when this request fails?

**Point:** A useful review looks beyond the happy path: empty states, retries, permissions, error messages, logging and handover.

**Evidence:** Show one before-and-after state from a product build.

**CTA:** Ask readers to test one failure path in their own product today.

### 9. Code ownership is part of the offer

**Hook:** Speed only helps if the team can keep moving afterwards.

**Point:** The repository, roadmap and technical decisions should remain understandable and usable by the client team.

**Evidence:** Show a handover note, repository structure or decision record that is safe to share.

**CTA:** Link to the product build or Monthly Studio route.

### 10. Fixed project or ongoing product team?

**Hook:** The right engagement depends on the uncertainty you still have.

**Point:** A fixed sprint suits a focused first version. A larger build suits a defined product that needs an end-to-end launch. Ongoing support suits a product with a queue of decisions and improvements.

**Evidence:** Use the three routes already named on the site.

**CTA:** Link to pricing.

### 11. A case study in one screen

**Hook:** One screen can show more than a list of technologies.

**Point:** Show the starting problem, the key product decision and the resulting workflow in one annotated image.

**Evidence:** Use Social Publisher, SignalMap or Ledger, and link to the full case study.

### 12. Invite a prototype review

**Hook:** If the prototype works in a demo but feels risky to release, that is a useful place to start.

**Point:** Explain what the visitor should send, what the audit covers and what they receive back.

**CTA:** “Get a Tiny Audit” with the direct link to `/tiny-audit`.

## Publishing rules

- Do not invent metrics, client quotes or delivery outcomes.
- Ask permission before naming a client, showing private screens or sharing a testimonial.
- Prefer one specific artefact over a list of technologies.
- Explain technical terms in the sentence where they appear.
- Keep the CTA useful and proportionate. The post should teach something even if nobody clicks.
- Reuse each strong case-study detail across LinkedIn, the relevant site page and the sales conversation.

## Measures to review monthly

- LinkedIn profile and post views.
- Clicks from LinkedIn to selected work, pricing and Tiny Audit.
- Tiny Audit starts and completed enquiries.
- Contact form completion rate.
- Which case studies are opened after a social visit.
- Qualified conversations that mention a specific post or case-study detail.

Review these as signals, not vanity targets. The aim is to learn which proof helps the right buyer take the next step.
